Glidex

Host resources

Besides vCPUs and memory, Glidex knows how each host is built: its NUMA nodes and cores, its hugepages, and its PCIe tree with the devices a VM could be given. It uses that to:

  • keep a VM’s memory, vCPUs and devices on one NUMA node, or split a GPU VM over exactly its GPUs’ NUMA nodes, and give it dedicated host CPUs;
  • count hugepages per node and page size, so VMs that need them get them;
  • hand out devices from pools by count, near each other, and size a VM that asks for GPUs from what each GPU brings;
  • prepare devices for passthrough, watch their health, and cordon the ones you don’t trust.

All of this works on a single host and in a cluster. On a single host the node is called local. Passing a device through by its address, and what the host needs for VFIO, is in PCI passthrough.

Host topology

Each node reads its topology from sysfs. A summary (NUMA nodes, cores, memory, hugepages, reserved CPUs, assignable devices) goes in the node’s record and is refreshed every two minutes, and right after a device is prepared or released. The full tree is read fresh on the node each time you ask for it.

gxctl node topology <node>             # NUMA nodes with CPUs, memory, hugepages and devices; reserved CPUs; warnings
gxctl node topology <node> --devices   # devices with NUMA node, root port, switch, link, VFs, PCIe errors
gxctl pci                              # this host's PCI devices, with NUMA node and readiness
gxctl pci 0000:41:00.0                 # one device, and why it isn't ready

<node> is a node name or id. In the web UI, a node’s page (Cluster → a node) has a Topology card: one column per NUMA node with its CPUs, memory, hugepages and devices, then devices with no NUMA node, the reserved CPUs, and the warnings.

ShownMeaning
CPUs, coresThe NUMA node’s CPUs, grouped by core (hyperthreads of one core together).
MemoryThe node’s memory, without its hugepage pool.
HugepagesPages per size on that NUMA node, less what OVS-DPDK takes there.
DevicesThe devices a VM could be given: GPUs, NICs, NVMe drives, accelerators, and anything bound to vfio-pci. Each with its class, ids, driver, IOMMU group, readiness and, with --devices, its root port, the PCIe switch above it, its link speed and width, SR-IOV VFs and PCIe error counts.
Reserved CPUsCPUs no VM gets: OVS-DPDK’s PMD and lcore CPUs, and cluster.node_reserved.cpu_list.

Reading a node’s topology needs a host role (auditor, net-admin or system-admin) on that host. Over the API it is GET /nodes/{id}/topology (read on that node) or GET /host/topology (the host that answers).

Ready devices

A device is ready for passthrough when:

  • the host has an IOMMU and the device has an IOMMU group;
  • it is bound to vfio-pci;
  • the other functions of its IOMMU group are on vfio-pci or unbound (bridges stay with the host);
  • the glidex user can open /dev/vfio/<group>, judged by the file’s owner, group and mode, as a VM’s unit would open it;
  • it isn’t an OVS-DPDK uplink;
  • no process other than Glidex’s own VMs has its IOMMU group open, such as a VM of libvirt or OpenNebula on the same host.

A device that isn’t says why:

ReasonFix
not bound to vfio-pci (driver: nvidia)Prepare it.
IOMMU group 30 also holds 0000:07:00.1 on snd_hda_intelPrepare it: the whole group goes to vfio-pci.
/dev/vfio/30 can't be opened by this userPrepare it (its udev rule gives the group device to glidex), or see PCI passthrough.
in use by qemu-kvm-one (pid 37226)Another hypervisor’s VM holds it. Being on vfio-pci doesn’t make it free. See Devices another hypervisor holds.
it is an OVS-DPDK uplinkNot for passthrough.

Pools hand out ready devices only. Who holds a group is asked of glidex-netd, which can see every process; without it, that check is skipped.

Warnings

The topology reports what it notices. Glidex never acts on these.

WarningMeaningWhat to do
iommu_offNo IOMMU groups.Turn on VT-d or AMD-Vi and the kernel option; see PCI passthrough.
numa_unknownA device has no NUMA node (firmware reports -1) on a host with several.A firmware setting or update. NUMA_POLICY_STRICT VMs can’t have it.
group_sharedA device shares its IOMMU group with a device of another class.Passing one through takes the other; move the card to another slot.
link_degradedA device’s PCIe link runs slower or narrower than it can. GPUs aren’t flagged: they slow their link when idle.Reseat the card, or check the slot.
hugepages_unbalancedA device sits on a NUMA node with no hugepages while other NUMA nodes have some.Reserve hugepages on that NUMA node too (see Hugepages).

NUMA nodes

On a host with several NUMA nodes, a VM is faster when its memory, its vCPUs and its devices are on one of them. A VM asks for that with numa_policy (see NUMA and dedicated CPUs):

  • NUMA_POLICY_PREFERRED: on one NUMA node if one fits, else anywhere on the host. Then the VM’s NumaAligned condition is False.
  • NUMA_POLICY_STRICT: on one NUMA node, or not on that host. It refuses devices on two NUMA nodes and devices with no NUMA node.
  • NUMA_POLICY_ALIGNED: on exactly the NUMA nodes of its devices, its vCPUs and memory split between them by device count, and the same layout shown to the guest. A GPU request sets it. Every device needs a NUMA node, and each node’s share must fit there (NUMA node 7: not enough memory (… of … MiB taken)).

Glidex picks the NUMA node with most of the VM’s devices, then the least allocated, then the lowest number. Each NUMA node’s room is its memory times cluster.scheduler.memory_overcommit, and its hugepages of each size. VMs bound to a NUMA node count there; VMs without a policy count on every NUMA node in proportion to its size.

The guest’s memory is bound to the NUMA node (to each node’s share, when aligned). Shared vCPUs still float over the whole host, so no node’s CPUs sit idle. The VM shows where it went: numa_node, or numa (one share per node) when aligned over several, and for dedicated vCPUs cpus. Before an aligned VM starts, each node is checked for its share of free hugepages.

In a cluster, a strict VM that fits no node waits as Unschedulable with each node’s reason (NUMA node 1: not enough memory (… of … MiB taken), its PCI devices are on NUMA nodes 0 and 1). On a single host the create is refused with 409. A change to a placed VM’s vCPUs, memory, policies, devices or networks is fitted again on its node; what no longer fits is refused with 409.

Dedicated CPUs

A VM with cpu_policy: CPU_POLICY_DEDICATED gets a host CPU of its own for each vCPU, on one NUMA node if it can (it implies NUMA_POLICY_PREFERRED). Its vCPU threads are pinned there; other VMs don’t run on them.

  • Whole cores. By default a dedicated VM takes both hyperthreads of each core, so no other VM shares a core with it. Turn this off with cluster.scheduler.dedicated_whole_cores: false to hand out single threads.
  • Isolated CPUs first. CPUs the kernel keeps tasks off (isolcpus, nohz_full) are used first.
  • Reserved CPUs are never given out: OVS-DPDK’s PMD and lcore CPUs, and by default the first core of each NUMA node, for the host. cluster.node_reserved.cpu_list replaces that default with your own list; an empty list reserves none.
  • Shared VMs give way. Once a node holds dedicated CPUs, VMs with shared vCPUs run only on the CPUs left, and running ones are moved when that set changes.
{
  "cluster": {
    "scheduler": { "dedicated_whole_cores": true },
    "node_reserved": { "cpu_list": "0-1,64-65" }
  }
}

cluster.node_reserved.cpu_list is per host: set it in each host’s /etc/glidex/control-plane.json. The reserved CPUs show in gxctl node topology. Restart the control plane after changing either key.

When no NUMA node has enough free CPUs, the reason says so (NUMA node 0: not enough free CPUs (8 dedicated vCPUs, 6 CPUs free)).

Hugepages

Hugepages are their own resource, counted per node and page size. Glidex doesn’t reserve them; the kernel does, at boot or later:

# kernel command line: reserve 1G pages at boot, before memory fragments
default_hugepagesz=1G hugepagesz=1G hugepages=64

# 2M pages, at run time, on NUMA node 1
echo 4096 | sudo tee /sys/devices/system/node/node1/hugepages/hugepages-2048kB/nr_hugepages

The node reads its pools per size and NUMA node (again within two minutes of a change). How they are counted:

  • What VMs may take is the pool less what OVS-DPDK takes for dpdk-socket-mem. Hugepages aren’t ordinary memory: a node’s allocatable memory leaves the pool out.
  • A VM with hugepages takes its whole memory in pages of its hugepage_size (2M or 1G; default: the host’s default size, usually 2M). They are never overcommitted, and a placed VM holds them whether it runs or not. Refusals: not enough 2M hugepages (2048 of 2048 MiB taken), no 1G hugepages, 1536 MiB is not a whole number of 1G pages.
  • A VM with a vhost-user NIC needs shared memory. It gets 2M pages without asking when the node has its memory plus a quarter (at least 256 MiB) to spare, and ordinary memory otherwise.
  • The VM’s page_kib says what it got: 4 for ordinary memory, else the page size in KiB.
  • Before a VM starts, its node checks that enough pages of the size are free (on its NUMA node, if it has one). If not, the start fails with the counts. Hugepages used outside Glidex show up this way.

On a single host, Glidex records the size of asked-for hugepages but doesn’t count them ahead; the check at start is what stops a VM. The size of vhost-user hugepages is decided at start too.

Device pools

A pool is a named set of devices, matched by ids or addresses. Instead of naming devices by address, a VM asks for a number of devices from a pool, and Glidex picks them. Pools are defined in /etc/glidex/control-plane.json (on each server, the same):

{
  "pci": {
    "pools": [
      { "name": "a100", "class": "gpu",
        "match": { "vendor_id": "0x10de", "device_id": "0x20b0" },
        "prepare": true,
        "per_device": { "vcpus": 14, "memory_mib": 57344,
                        "hugepages": true, "cpu_policy": "dedicated" } },
      { "name": "cx7", "class": "nic",
        "match": { "vendor_id": "0x15b3", "device_id": "0x1021" },
        "nodes": ["gpu-01", "gpu-02"] }
    ],
    "allow": [
      { "pool": "a100", "tenants": ["research"] },
      { "pool": "cx7", "projects": ["ml-team"] }
    ]
  }
}
KeyMeaning
nameThe pool’s name. Unique.
classA label for people and usage reports: gpu, nic, nvme, dpu, accelerator. gpu also marks the pools a GPU request can use without naming one, and that the web UI’s create form sizes VMs from.
matchvendor_id, device_id, class_code (or its start, 0x0302) and addresses. Every key given must match. A pool must have a match. With vf_of (itself a match of physical functions), the pool takes those functions’ SR-IOV virtual functions; see SR-IOV virtual functions.
nodesNode names or ids the pool is on. Default: every node.
prepareEach node binds its devices of the pool to vfio-pci when glidex starts (see Prepare devices).
per_deviceWhat a VM asking for gpus from the pool gets per device, unless it sizes itself: vcpus and memory_mib (both required), and optionally hugepages (true/false) and cpu_policy (shared or dedicated). See GPU requests.

The control plane refuses to start with two pools of the same name, a pool without a match, two pools with the same match on the same nodes, or a grant naming an unknown pool. Restart the control plane after changing pools or grants.

Who may use a pool

A pci.allow entry with pool grants the pool to projects (ids or names) or tenants (ids or slugs), like device grants (see Who may use a device). net-admin and system-admin may use any pool. In policies the permission is usePciPool.

How VMs get devices

A VM asks with devices, for example [{"pool": "a100", "count": 2, "same_switch": true}] (in gxctl create, a100:2:switch; in the web UI’s VM form, Device Pools). Glidex picks devices of the pool that are ready, not cordoned and not held by another VM (whole IOMMU groups counted), on the NUMA node of the VM’s other devices first, then by address. With same_switch, all of them sit under one PCIe switch, the one the VM already uses if it can.

The picks are shown on the VM as pool_devices and are passed through at its next start. They are claims like devices named by address: the VM keeps them while it exists. When a pool is short, the reason says why: pool a100: 1 of 2 free, pool a100: no 2 free devices under one PCIe switch, pool a100 is not on this node. In a cluster the VM waits for a node; on a single host the request is refused with 409.

GPU requests

A user can ask for GPUs by count instead of sizing the VM: "gpus": {"count": 4, "pool": "a100"} (in gxctl create, 4:a100; in the web UI, a GPU pool’s count). Glidex turns it into:

  • a pool request for 4 devices of a100;
  • NUMA_POLICY_ALIGNED, so the VM spans exactly its GPUs’ NUMA nodes (see NUMA nodes);
  • 4 × the pool’s per_device vCPUs and memory, its hugepages and its cpu_policy.

What the VM’s request gives itself wins. Without pool, the project’s only usable pool of class gpu is taken (cluster or tenant pool). A tenant pool sizes like its cluster pool. Quotas, grants and metering are those of the pool. Users see the sizing in the create form and as per_device_vcpus and per_device_memory_mib in the project’s pool listing. The refusals are in the user guide’s GPUs.

Size per_device so the GPUs of one NUMA node fit that node: its cores less the reserved ones, and its memory less the host’s share, divided by its GPUs. For a node with 16 cores (32 threads), one core reserved and 2 GPUs: 15 cores leave 7 each, so 14 vCPUs with whole cores, and about 56 GiB each out of 126 GiB. A VM that asks for more than a node can give is refused there, naming the node.

"nvlink": true is refused for now: NVLink between passed-through GPUs on NVSwitch boards needs a fabric Glidex doesn’t run yet.

See what pools hold

gxctl pci pools                 # each pool's devices per node: free, held by a VM, cordoned or not ready
gxctl pci pools --project lab   # the pools project lab may use, with free counts

The full listing (GET /pci-pools) needs a host role. With --project (GET /pci-pools?project=<p>), anyone who can read the project sees the pools it may use and how many devices are free, without addresses or holders; it lists the project’s tenant pools too.

Quotas

A project quota devices.<pool> limits how many devices of a pool the project’s VMs ask for, counted like vcpus over all its VMs:

gxctl project quota lab devices.a100=2

Over the limit, a request is refused with quota_exceeded (resource devices.a100). A tenant’s cap limits the same over all its projects (tenant_quota_exceeded). A pool without a quota is unlimited. In the web UI, the project’s Quotas and usage card shows Devices: <pool> for each pool with a limit or in use, and Edit quotas offers a field for each pool the project may use. See Projects.

Tenant pools

A tenant granted a pool can split its share among its projects. Its tenant-admin creates tenant pools, each drawing from a cluster pool, with a maximum and the projects that may use it:

gxctl tenant device-pool set acme team-a-gpus --from a100 --max 4 --projects team-a,team-a-ci
gxctl tenant device-pools acme                 # pool, cluster pool, held/max, free, projects
gxctl tenant device-pool remove acme team-a-gpus

In the web UI it is the tenant page’s Device pools card. Over the API, PUT /tenants/{id}/device-pools replaces the tenant’s whole set.

  • The name is 1-63 lowercase letters, digits and dashes, and can’t be a cluster pool’s name. --from must be a cluster pool granted to the tenant, --max at least 1, and the projects the tenant’s.
  • A VM of one of those projects asks for {"pool": "team-a-gpus"}. It gets devices of a100, and the VM shows "pool": "a100", "tenant_pool": "team-a-gpus". Other projects are refused (403).
  • The tenant pool’s projects together ask for at most max devices (tenant_quota_exceeded, resource tenant_pool.team-a-gpus). The tenant’s cap and the project quota on a100 still apply.
  • If the tenant loses its grant on a100, the tenant pool shows ungranted and gives nothing new. VMs that hold devices keep them, as do VMs of a tenant pool you remove.

Usage

Metering counts device.alloc.<pool>: the devices of a pool a VM holds, in device-hours, from its start to its exit (paused too). Devices of a tenant pool count under the cluster pool’s name. Devices named by address aren’t metered. See Usage.

Prepare devices

Glidex can bind a device to vfio-pci for you, on any node:

gxctl node prepare-device <node> 0000:41:00.0   # its whole IOMMU group to vfio-pci
gxctl node release-device <node> 0000:41:00.0   # back on the drivers it had

In the web UI, the node’s Topology card has Prepare and Release next to each device. Over the API: POST /nodes/{id}/devices/{address}/prepare and .../release.

Preparing:

  • binds every function of the device’s IOMMU group to vfio-pci. Bridges stay with the host;
  • remembers the drivers they had, for release;
  • writes /etc/udev/rules.d/90-glidex-vfio-<group>.rules, which gives /dev/vfio/<group> to the glidex group with mode 0660, and applies it at once;
  • refuses a group holding a NIC the host uses (one with addresses or routes), an OVS-DPDK uplink, or a device without an IOMMU group.

The host’s driver lets go of the devices: a GPU on nvidia stops being the host’s. Release works with any function of the group, and refuses a device Glidex didn’t prepare.

Release refuses a group that a process has open, a VM’s included (IOMMU group 30 is in use by qemu-kvm-one (pid 37226)), and so does a change to a PF’s VF count while its group or a VF’s is open.

Preparing and releasing need net-admin or system-admin on that host. A prepared device goes back to its usual driver at reboot. For devices that should always be ready:

  • give their pool prepare: true: each node then prepares its devices of the pool that aren’t ready when glidex starts, skipping devices without an IOMMU group and OVS-DPDK uplinks. A device that can’t be prepared stays not ready, and the control plane’s log says why;
  • or bind them at boot, before glidex and any other hypervisor starts, with the installer’s --vfio-devices (and keep VF counts with --sriov); see Keep it across reboots.

Device health and cordoning

Each node reads its devices’ PCIe error counters (AER: correctable, non-fatal, fatal). gxctl node topology <node> --devices shows them as aer correctable/nonfatal/fatal, and the Topology card marks a device with uncorrectable errors (PCIe errors).

Glidex watches the devices of running VMs. If one falls off the bus, or has new uncorrectable errors since the VM started, the VM’s DeviceHealthy condition turns False with the reason, and a DeviceError event is recorded once (gxctl events <vm>). Glidex doesn’t stop the VM or take the device away. Errors inside the guest (an NVIDIA Xid, say) only the guest sees.

To keep a device you don’t trust from new VMs, cordon it:

gxctl node cordon-device <node> 0000:41:00.0
gxctl node uncordon-device <node> 0000:41:00.0

Or Cordon and Uncordon on the Topology card, where a cordoned device is marked cordoned. Over the API: POST /nodes/{id}/devices/{address}/cordon and .../uncordon.

A cordoned device is refused to new VMs by address (PCI device 0000:41:00.0 is cordoned), and pools skip it. The VM that holds it keeps it. Cordoning needs net-admin or system-admin on that host. The node records its cordoned devices, so they stay cordoned across restarts.
